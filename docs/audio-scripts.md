# Step Narration Scripts

Scripts for the 10 activities that don't have audio yet. Once recorded, drop the
mp3s in `public/assets/` and I'll wire each one up (`audioSrc` + `stepTimes`).

## How to record

- **One mp3 per activity**, named exactly as listed (e.g. `wiggle-cool-down.mp3`).
- **Start speaking step 1 immediately** — the player expects step 1 at 0:00
  (no intro music or greeting first).
- **Leave a clear 1.5–2 second pause between steps.** I'll use those gaps to set
  the timestamps that sync the on-screen steps to your voice.
- Tone reference: your existing Freeze Feelings / Tense and Relax recordings —
  warm, unhurried, talking to the parent (the child hears it too).
- Numbers in parentheses are spoken out loud, so "(10, 9, 8)" is read as
  "ten… nine… eight".

## emotion-charades.mp3 (6 steps)

1. Write feeling words — or draw simple faces — on slips of paper: happy, sad, angry, scared, surprised, excited… and drop them in a bowl.
2. Take turns drawing a slip — no peeking, keep it secret!
3. Act out the feeling using only your face and body. No words or sounds allowed!
4. The other player guesses the feeling. Close guesses count — the goal is talking about feelings, not winning.
5. After each round, chat about it. What clues gave it away? When have you felt that way?
6. Swap roles and keep going — and add trickier feelings like proud, jealous, or nervous as you warm up.

## whats-my-temperature.mp3 (6 steps)

1. Draw a thermometer together — cool blue at the bottom, warm red at the top.
2. Cool means calm and relaxed. Hot means a big, strong feeling.
3. Ask your child: where are you on the thermometer right now?
4. Talk about it — what's making you feel that way? Name the feeling together.
5. Feeling hot? Pick one way to cool down: a few deep breaths, a stretch, or a quiet break.
6. Check the thermometer again. Did your temperature change?

## wiggle-cool-down.mp3 (7 steps)

1. Find your space. Have your child pick a spot where they can move around freely, without bumping into furniture or anyone else.
2. Set up the idea. Tell them: we're going to count down from ten to one, and our bodies will do different things at each number, to help us go from wiggly… to calm.
3. Ten, nine, eight — wiggle it out! Both of you stand up and wiggle your whole bodies, making silly noises. The sillier the better.
4. Seven, six — start quieting down. Keep moving, but drop your voice to a whisper as you count.
5. Five, four — sit down and go silent. Sit down together, and stop talking completely.
6. Three — take a deep breath and roll your shoulders back. Two — another deep breath, and cover your eyes. Then count down through one… and zero.
7. Open your eyes and check in. Ask: how did you feel as we went from fast and wiggly to slow and calm? Was there a moment when you felt yourself relax?

## musical-drawings.mp3 (6 steps)

1. Grab paper and markers, and pick a song with a clear mood.
2. Start the music, and let your marker move however the music feels — fast, slow, loopy, or sharp.
3. When a line goes somewhere you didn't mean it to… don't erase it.
4. Turn that "oops" line into something — a cloud, a wave, a creature — part of the picture.
5. Switch to a song with a different mood, and keep going on the same page.
6. When the music stops, look at your art together. Which mistakes became your favorite parts?

## balloon-breathing.mp3 (6 steps)

1. Sit comfortably or lie down, and place both hands gently on your belly.
2. Imagine your belly is a balloon, in your favorite color.
3. Breathe in slowly through your nose, and feel the balloon fill up — hands rising.
4. Pause for a moment at the top. The balloon is full!
5. Breathe out slowly through your mouth, and feel the balloon gently deflate.
6. Repeat five times. Notice how your body feels when the balloon is soft and empty.

## flower-breathing.mp3 (6 steps)

1. Sit together somewhere comfy, and imagine holding a beautiful flower.
2. What flower is it? What color? Let your child describe it.
3. Bring the flower up to your nose and smell it — a slow, deep breath in through your nose.
4. Pause a moment… enjoy the smell.
5. Let the breath out with a soft, happy sigh — ahhh.
6. Smell the flower five more times, slower each time. Notice how your body softens.

## bubble-breathing.mp3 (6 steps)

1. Grab your bubbles and find a good spot — outside, or over a towel, works well.
2. First, let them blow however they want. Watch what happens with fast, hard puffs!
3. Now try it slow. Take a deep breath in through your nose.
4. Blow out slowly, gently, and steadily — and watch the bubble grow big.
5. Talk about it: which breath made the best bubbles? Fast, or slow?
6. Finish with three pretend bubbles — same slow breath, no wand. That's your calm-down breath!

## feelings-circle.mp3 (6 steps)

1. Sit in a circle, or around the table, where everyone can see each other.
2. Explain the one rule: everyone gets a turn, all feelings are okay, and passing is allowed.
3. Go around: how are you feeling right now? Little ones can point to a feeling face, or show it with their own face.
4. Take your own turn honestly — share a real feeling from your day.
5. If someone wants to say more — "I feel excited because…" — make space. But never push.
6. Close the circle with a breath together… and do it again tomorrow. The magic is in the repetition.

## emotion-portraits.mp3 (6 steps)

1. Set out paper and markers, and pick four or five feelings to draw — start with happy, sad, angry, surprised, scared.
2. Before drawing each one, make the face together in a mirror. What are your eyebrows doing? Your mouth?
3. Draw a portrait of that feeling — a face, a whole person, or even just colors and shapes.
4. Compare the portraits. What's different between the angry face and the surprised face?
5. Talk about a time your child felt each one. Where did they feel it in their body?
6. Hang the portraits up — they become a feelings chart your child can point to on big-feeling days.

## mood-diary.mp3 (6 steps)

1. Pick a notebook together and decorate the cover — it should feel like theirs, not homework.
2. Choose a regular moment for a one-minute entry — like right before bed.
3. Each entry gets three tiny things: today's strongest feeling, what was happening, and where they felt it in their body.
4. Younger writers can draw a face, and dictate the rest to you.
5. Once a week, read back through together like detectives. Do any patterns show up?
6. Spot one pattern, and make one small plan. Grumpy before dinner? Snack at four thirty.
